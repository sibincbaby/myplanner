### 1. AWS CloudTrail (event history, multi-region org trails, Athena-over-S3 log queries, CloudTrail Lake where enabled) `closed`

**Why it's powerful:** CloudTrail records every API call across every AWS account, region and service: who did what, from which IP and role session, through which assumed-role chain. For change forensics, incident response, compliance evidence and answering 'what broke prod?', it is the ground truth.

**Why people bounce off it:** The console's Event history only shows management events from the last 90 days, one region at a time, with a single attribute filter. Real questions span accounts and regions and need Athena DDL over partitioned S3 log prefixes, or CloudTrail Lake SQL against nested JSON (userIdentity.sessionContext.sessionIssuer.arn, requestParameters blobs). To find the human behind an action you have to walk AssumeRole and SSO chains by hand. Most engineers give up and ask the one security person who knows how.

**AI operator:** A 'Who Changed It' forensic operator. It knows the trail topology (org trail bucket, partition layout, Lake event data stores) and turns plain questions into LookupEvents calls, Athena SQL or Lake SQL, choosing the cheapest path for the time range and scope. It resolves assumed-role and Identity Center sessions back to a named person, and returns a readable timeline with a diff of what changed (rebuilt from requestParameters plus AWS Config history). Any query can be saved as a scheduled detective check.

**Killer example:** Mid-incident, the on-call engineer types: 'Checkout started 500ing at 14:07. What changed in any prod account in the 30 minutes before, and who did it?' The AI picks Lake SQL because the window is narrow and spans 14 accounts, filters out 41,000 read-only calls, and returns 4 write events on a timeline. The prime suspect is ModifyDBParameterGroup at 14:05, which cut max_connections on checkout-db from 500 to 100. The AI traces it through the CI role's session name to GitHub Actions run #4412 and the merged PR. A second event, a manual RevokeSecurityGroupIngress by jane@corp through the PowerUser SSO permission set, gets flagged as unrelated. The AI shows a before/after diff from AWS Config and offers a one-click revert of the parameter, drafting the incident-ticket note itself. Elapsed time is about 40 seconds, where the manual route means 14 console tabs and an hour of Athena.

**Integration path:** AWS SDK/CLI: cloudtrail lookup-events, CloudTrail Lake StartQuery/GetQueryResults, Athena StartQueryExecution over the org-trail S3 bucket with tool-generated partition-projection DDL, AWS Config GetResourceConfigHistory for before/after state, and Identity Center plus STS for identity resolution. Runs read-only through a cross-account audit role. Ships as an MCP server plus a Slack bot.

**Demand 5/5 · Buildability 4/5**

### 2. Shopee Seller Centre (Shopee Open Platform API v2), with Lazada Seller Center (Lazada Open Platform) as a second connector `closed`

**Why it's powerful:** Shopee is Southeast Asia's largest marketplace, with millions of active sellers. Seller Centre covers multi-tier variations, shop, follow-prize and product vouchers, Add-On Deals, Bundle Deals, Shop Flash Sales, registration for the double-date mega-sales (9.9, 10.10, 11.11, 12.12), per-SKU logistics channels, Shopee Ads (Search, Discovery, GMV Max) and multi-shop/cross-border (CNSC/global SKU) management.

**Why people bounce off it:** Every promotion type has its own page and rules: price floors, stacking, overlap bans (for example, a Flash Sale and a Discount can't share an item), and campaign stock that must be reserved ahead of time. Before each mega-sale, sellers spend days cloning discounts SKU by SKU across 2 to 6 shops. Copying listings across shops and countries means re-mapping category attributes and logistics. The ads console hides ROAS by item. Lazada has the same pain with different vocabulary, and most SEA sellers run both platforms.

**AI operator:** A 'Mega-Sale Operator' with skills over catalog (get_item_list, update_price, update_stock, add_model), discounts, vouchers, bundle and add-on deals, shop flash sales, logistics and ads endpoints. A rules checker validates price floors, promotion overlaps and stock reservations before any write. The whole campaign is planned as a dry-run diff table for approval, executed with rate-limit-aware batching, and reverted after the sale to a saved snapshot. Cross-shop and cross-platform cloning uses LLM attribute mapping, with a human checking low-confidence fields.

**Killer example:** A skincare seller with shops in Malaysia and Singapore plus a Lazada store says: '11.11 plan: 15% off all Skincare everywhere except anything that drops below 20% margin, buy-2-save-10% bundle on toner plus serum, midnight flash sale on our top 5 sellers capped at 50 units each, and put it all back to normal on the 13th.' The AI reads unit costs from the linked Google Sheet and works through 1,140 SKUs. It excludes 37 that would fall under the margin floor and catches 3 items already locked in a Shopee platform campaign, which would make the discount fail silently. It also flags 9 SKUs where 15% would break Shopee's lowest-price-in-30-days rule. The result is a 214-row diff grouped by shop and platform, with the projected margin at each discount. After a single approval it creates the discounts, bundle and flash-sale slots on both platforms, reserves the campaign stock, and schedules the revert for 13 Nov 00:00. On the morning of the 12th it posts a sell-through summary with a suggestion to raise the flash cap on 2 items that sold out in 11 minutes.

**Integration path:** Shopee Open Platform v2 REST API (partner_id plus HMAC-signed requests, shop-level OAuth; modules: product, discount, voucher, bundle_deal, add_on_deal, shop_flash_sale, logistics, ads, global_product). Lazada Open Platform REST/SDK is the second connector. MVP: Partner app registered as an ISV, a Postgres snapshot store for rollbacks, and an LLM tool layer over the modules. The main friction is rate limits and app-review approval.

### 3. Microsoft Word wildcard Find & Replace (with Google Docs/Apps Script regex and LibreOffice ICU regex as second connectors): the 'bulk text surgery' layer `closed`

**Why it's powerful:** Word's Advanced Find with 'Use wildcards' is a full pattern engine: capture groups, backreferences \1 to \9, character classes, counts {1,3}, word anchors < >, and special codes such as ^p, ^t and ^#. It can also match on formatting and styles and apply formatting on replace, so one pass can restructure a 300-page contract or thesis. Google Docs (Apps Script findText/replaceText) and LibreOffice (ICU regex) offer comparable engines.

**Why people bounce off it:** Word's wildcard syntax isn't standard regex: @ instead of +, < > for word boundaries, ^13 instead of ^p in wildcard mode, no lazy quantifiers and no alternation, and special characters must be escaped. Format-only find/replace is hidden behind 'More >> Format'. Most users don't know the feature exists. Those who try it hit 'too complex' errors or wreck the document with one unpreviewed Replace All, then go back to editing 2,000 instances by hand. Google Docs' UI regex can't replace with capture groups at all.

**AI operator:** The user describes a transformation in plain English. The AI compiles it to a find spec (wildcard or regex pattern plus format and style filters) and a per-match replacement function. A dry run lists every match with before/after context, and the user can approve all or untick individual ones. Changes are applied as tracked revisions, so the job stays reviewable and undoable. Skills: a pattern library (dates, citations, names, currency, legal cross-refs, figure numbers), a regex-to-Word-wildcard translator, format-aware matching, and batch runs across a folder of .docx files.

**Killer example:** A paralegal has a 280-page master services agreement and says: 'Convert every date like 03/14/2026 to 14 March 2026, flip "Smith, John" names in the signature blocks to "John Smith", bold every cross-reference like "Section 4.2(b)", and leave the tables alone.' The AI compiles three patterns, excludes 41 table ranges, and shows 212 previewed matches. Three are flagged for a human: '04/05/2026' in a clause that cites a UK court, and two 'Section' references that point to sections that don't exist. On approval every change lands as a tracked change authored 'AI Operator', so the partner can accept all 209 safe edits in one click and decide the 3 flagged ones individually. That is about 20 minutes of review instead of an afternoon of Ctrl+H.

**Integration path:** In-app: an Office.js Word add-in. body.search(pattern, {matchWildcards:true, ...}) returns a RangeCollection, and replacements are computed client-side because insertText can't expand \1. Writes go through range.insertText(..., 'Replace') and range.font/style with changeTrackingMode on, which works on Word desktop, web and Mac. A Windows COM/VBA path (Range.Find.Execute with '\1') handles native bulk runs. Offline folder batches use python-docx/docx4j with run-merging. Google Docs uses an Apps Script add-on, and LibreOffice uses a UNO replace descriptor with SearchRegularExpression. The LLM only has to emit a small, checkable pattern DSL.

**Demand 4/5 · Buildability 4/5**

### 5. Amazon DynamoDB (tables, GSIs/LSIs, capacity modes, Streams, TTL, PartiQL, Contributor Insights) `closed`

**Why it's powerful:** DynamoDB is the serverless key-value/document database behind a huge number of AWS workloads. It offers single-digit-millisecond latency at any scale, on-demand or provisioned capacity with autoscaling, global tables, PITR, Streams into Lambda, TTL and zero-ETL exports.

**Why people bounce off it:** Access-pattern-first single-table design (PK/SK overloading, GSI overloading, sparse indexes) is unintuitive for SQL people. The console item explorer can't express real queries. KeyConditionExpression/FilterExpression syntax with ExpressionAttributeNames/Values placeholders is error-prone. Scans quietly cost a fortune. Hot partitions, throttling and GSI back-pressure are hard to diagnose, and both the capacity-mode choice and adding a GSI to a live table are risky guesswork.

**AI operator:** A 'DynamoDB Copilot' with four skill areas. (1) Access-pattern modeling: plain-language queries become a PK/SK/GSI design, proven with sample items. (2) Safe querying: questions become a Query (never a Scan unless confirmed) with correct expressions, an RCU cost estimate and pagination. (3) Operations: it explains throttling, hot keys and capacity-mode savings from CloudWatch and Contributor Insights. (4) Migrations: it plans GSI additions and backfills.

**Killer example:** A backend dev asks: 'Show me all unpaid orders for customer 4821 from the last 30 days, and why does the orders table throttle at 9am every day?' The AI sees that GSI1 (GSI1PK=CUST#4821, GSI1SK begins_with ORDER#2026-09) can serve the query. It runs that with a status filter for about 3 RCU, and notes that the Scan the dev would have written costs about 41,000 RCU. From Contributor Insights it finds that 70% of 9am writes hit a single key, DATE#today, written by a daily batch Lambda. It proposes write sharding (DATE#today#0..9) with the exact code diff for the Lambda and the matching read fan-out. Separately, it shows from 14 days of consumed capacity that on-demand would cost $212 a month less for this table, and it waits for confirmation and a PITR check before switching.

**Integration path:** DynamoDB data and control plane APIs via the AWS SDK (Query, ExecuteStatement/PartiQL, DescribeTable, UpdateTable, UpdateContinuousBackups), CloudWatch GetMetricData plus Contributor Insights, and Cost Explorer. DynamoDB Local provides a sandbox mode. Writes are gated behind confirmation and a PITR check. Ships as a desktop/web app or MCP server.

**Demand 4/5 · Buildability 4/5**

### 6. Amazon API Gateway (REST API v1 + HTTP API v2 + WebSocket APIs) `closed`

**Why it's powerful:** API Gateway is the default front door for serverless and microservice backends on AWS. It handles managed auth (IAM, Cognito, JWT, Lambda authorizers), throttling, usage plans and API keys, VTL mapping, stages and canary deployments, custom domains, mTLS, private APIs, WAF attachment and access logging.

**Why people bounce off it:** There are two incompatible products (REST and HTTP) with different feature sets. Every route change needs a separate 'Deploy to stage' step that people forget. CORS is configured per method, and OPTIONS mocks plus 4XX/5XX gateway responses are needed or browsers fail silently. VTL templates are arcane. Base-path mappings, authorizer cache TTLs and stage variables interact in confusing ways, and 'Missing Authentication Token' 403s give almost no diagnostic.

**AI operator:** An 'API Edge Operator'. It exports the live API as OpenAPI with x-amazon-apigateway extensions, explains it in plain language, and makes changes as a reviewed OpenAPI diff before deploying to a named stage. Skills: CORS repair, authorizer wiring, usage-plan and API-key provisioning, VTL generation from example input/output JSON, canary setup, and 403/502/504 debugging by correlating execution and access logs.

**Killer example:** A frontend lead pastes a browser console error and says: 'Our React app gets CORS errors on /orders in prod, and our partner Acme needs its own key capped at 50 req/s.' The AI finds the two real causes: POST /orders has no OPTIONS method, and the DEFAULT_4XX gateway response lacks Access-Control-Allow-Origin, so every auth failure looks like a CORS failure. It also notices the last route change was never deployed to prod, which is why staging works. It patches both for app.corp.com, creates usage plan 'partners-50rps' (burst 100), issues an API key for Acme bound to the prod stage, and deploys with a change description. It then runs a curl preflight and a real POST to prove the fix, with the before/after OpenAPI diff attached for the PR.

**Integration path:** apigateway and apigatewayv2 APIs via the AWS SDK: GetExport/PutRestApi (OpenAPI with AWS extensions), CreateDeployment, UpdateStage canarySettings, CreateUsagePlan/CreateApiKey, UpdateGatewayResponse, TestInvokeMethod for dry runs, and CloudWatch Logs Insights for execution logs. Changes are staged as OpenAPI diffs, optionally emitted as SAM/CDK/Terraform patches.

**Demand 4/5 · Buildability 4/5**

### 11. Oracle Analytics Publisher (formerly BI Publisher), the reporting engine in Oracle Fusion Cloud, E-Business Suite, PeopleSoft, JD Edwards and Oracle Analytics Server/Cloud `closed`

**Why it's powerful:** Analytics Publisher produces nearly every customer-facing and regulatory document in the Oracle ERP world: AP checks with remittance stubs, AR invoices, dunning letters, payslips, 1099s and bank payment files. One data model plus a layout can burst thousands of personalised PDFs, emails or SFTP drops, and eText templates generate fixed-width and delimited bank formats.

**Why people bounce off it:** Layouts are Word RTF files with XSL logic hidden in form fields (<?for-each-group:...?>, <?if:...?>, running totals, sub-templates, overflow section breaks). eText templates are tables of positional rules. Data models (.xdm) mix SQL datasets, LOVs, parameters, triggers and a separate bursting query. Finance users can't move a logo without a consultant, and one misplaced tag yields a blank PDF with no useful error.

**AI operator:** A 'Layout Operator' for analysts and functional consultants. It pulls the report's sample XML and data model and explains the existing template in plain English. It then writes or modifies the RTF/eText/XDM, uploads it to a sandbox catalog folder, runs it with real parameters, and rasterises the PDF. It checks the output visually and structurally (group counts, totals that reconcile) before offering to promote.

**Killer example:** User says: 'On the AP check, cap each remittance stub at 15 invoices and push the overflow onto an attachment page.
